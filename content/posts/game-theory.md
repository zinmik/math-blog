The rapid development of AI's mathematical capabilities, especially the October 6 OpenAI math drop, left mathematicians scrambling for some kind of response. The uncertainties that people have can be roughly divided along the lines of two main questions, “Where are we headed?” and “What should we do about it?”. A mathematically trained person will talk about what they know, which is why the discussion is not couched in terms of history and philosophy of mathematics. But the questions are non-mathematical, belonging to these disciplines much more than to math itself. Any comprehensive analysis of the situation must have two parts, descriptive (answering the first question) and prescriptive (answering the second). It seems to me that the banner of “the goal of mathematics is human understanding” that so many have flocked around is the latter much more than the former.

This was my motivation for making a foray into history and reading *The World the Game Theorists Made*, a book by Paul Erickson. My initial thought was that there could be some analogies between mathematicians deciding to leave academia to work on AI safety in the private sector and mathematicians working on military applications of game theory in institutions such as the RAND Corporation during the Cold War. Of course, plenty of math students get their degree and go on to work for a private company. The difference here is that the RAND cadre continued doing conventional mathematical research with output consisting largely of journal articles and reports. The purpose of this post is to summarize some of what I learned and see how well the analogy holds up.

The origins of military applications of game theory can be traced to operations research, a “set of activities connected with assessing the effectiveness of new technologies on the battlefield”. Since the early 1940s, it was conducted under the National Defense Research Comittee (NDRC), soon absorbed into the Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD). Vannevar Bush, the primary founder of these organizations, established the Applied Mathematics Panel (AMP) of the NDRC in 1942 for the purpose of dealing with mathematical problems of different branches of the military in a centralized way. Here is a quote from the book that stuck with me:
> The AMP in particular helped to cultivate a new generation of practical mathematicians, men and women who were talented managers and salespeople, and who had fewer qualms about violating disciplinary norms.

These included Mina Rees, who later became an influential figure in scientific funding and a champion of women in science.

A major contract for the AMP, unprecedented in its scope, came from the Army Air Forces in the spring of 1944. Assigned the number AC-92, its aim was to assess the capabilities and the most effective uses of the B-29 airplane. Game theory was first mentioned in this context by Merrill Flood, the executive secretary of the AC-92's steering committee, “to solve a highly simplified model of aerial combat involving an offensive bomber and a fighter plane”. This seems to be a typical example of the applications of game theory in the early days.

Project RAND originated in October 1945 right after the end of World War II as a contract between the Air Force and the Douglas Aircraft Company based in Santa Monica, California. In 1948, it was reorganized and the contract was transferred to the newly established RAND Corporation. With the objective to conduct research of continental warfare, it enjoyed unprecedented levels of funding. The RAND mathematicians did pioneering work in game theory, especially the theory of the two-person zero-sum game, and related areas such as optimization, linear and dynamic programming, and computational mathematics. I remember that when I read Silvia Nasar's book *A Beautiful Mind* as a teenager, I was impressed by the description of the laid-back work culture, with no fixed working hours, long communal lunches, game playing, and pranks. The history of the company's subsequent development is too complicated for me to trace here. Suffice it to say that RAND remained a powerhouse throughout the 1950s and into the 1960s.

Instead of continuing the historical review, let us focus on the similarities between game theory and the newly developing mathematics of AI safety. What makes the work at RAND immediately stand apart from most of mathematical research is the potential for direct practical applications. During the Cold War, there was a pervasive feeling of a looming crisis, and mathematicians perhaps thought themselves uniquely equipped to deal with this crisis. Meeting the challenge required the adoption of the new framework of game theory (coincidentally, the mathematics of AI safety might also [include game theory](https://maisi.org/research) but that is not the basis for our analogy). The mathematics of AI safety will inevitably have connections to other disciplines (computer science, statistics, ethics...), as did game theory. One can expect that the research will receive a lot of funding, with a significant portion of it coming from private sources.

The members of the younger generation returning from the war often found that there was no place for them in the rigid academic structures, so they jumped at the opportunity to try something different. My impression is that getting a permanent faculty position is harder now than it was a decade ago, even though I do not have data to support this view. In any case, I expect that plenty of early-career mathematicians will happily go to work in AI safety instead of trying to hang on at university departments, especially with the whole practice of traditional research being thrown into question. And besides, as the Cold War era game theorists demonstrated, mathematical careers in the private sector and academia are not mutually exclusive.

That is not to say that the current situation is exactly the same. Project RAND was founded to continue work that started during World War II, and we have not experienced anything comparable in recent past. Rather, there is a sense of global events gathering speed, with the major crisis lying ahead. Another difference is that the foundational text of game theory, von Neumann and Morgenster's *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, was not published with military applications in mind. But it was ready at hand when mathematicians working in operations research went looking for a theory that could be useful. Under different historical circumstances, they might have zoned in on some other branch of mathematics instead. As far as I know, there is no comparable text on the mathematics of AI safety, and it is not even clear what exactly will it comprise. It will have to be created specifically for the intended purpose.

To what extent can the observations about the historical development of game theory be generalized? In order to answer this question, we would first need to identify and examine other instances when mathematics was faced with an important, vaguely defined problem. But assuming that our analogy holds, we can imagine a possible scenario for what is going to happen with the mathematics of AI safety. One set of subproblems will prove more accessible to analysis than others. This will spawn a new theory, for example an “AI alignment theory” or some such, which will become the dominant idiom of the field (just as the two-person zero-sum game dominated early game theory). There will be a flurry of mathematical papers but the theoretical results will often prove hard to implement in practice (the fact that a combat between two planes can be modeled as a game does not easily translate to recommendations for the pilot). To enable practical applications, mathematicians will need to extend the idiom by drawing on other techniques that were originally not part of it (optimization, convex geometry, linear programming in the case of game theory). Interdisciplinarity will be crucial. Inherent limitations of the approach will be gradually discovered (the theory of a two-person zero-sum game could not be satisfactorily extended to an $n$-person game). Eventually, the center of mass will shift somewhere else and the original theory will start playing a lesser role.

If I decide to dabble in history again, I would like to take a look at the way mathematicians behave in response to a crisis as a group. It seems to me that while we can generally agree on which theorems have been proven and which have not, we cannot agree on pretty much anything else. Perhaps it is possible to argue that mathematician's attitudes during both world wars were determined primarily by their nationality rather than by some common set of values. I am dimly aware of an incident when David Hilbert and other German mathematicians got banned from the ICM after World War I, which sounds like a good place to start.
