Some big names in mathematics are clearly concerned about the current developments regarding AI. For example, Terrence Tao published 39 posts on his blog in September, compared to 6 in August. Most of these are guest features by people who might be called “leading mathematicians”, to the extent that our profession resembles a hierarchical structure with any sort of leadership. They are overwhelmingly tenured professors based at prestigious institution in the US. In many of the posts, their authors adhere to some version of the idea that the purpose of mathematics is not producing proofs but human understanding, which they pursue at the frontier of their respective areas. Most of them don't seem to acknowledge that there were any problems with mathematical research before AI came along. There are a few exceptions, such as this [post](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/13/deep-theorems-were-scarce-and-difficult-and-so-became-an-effective-mechanism-to-identify-deep-thought-ai-has-broken-this-system/#more-18208) by Bryna Kra, where she says on the subject:

*“All of these issues existed before AI entered our field. Unfortunately, we no longer have the luxury of addressing them in a leisurely manner.”*

People outside of mathematics may have the impression that all the work we do revolves around a handful of famous problems, such as the Riemann hypothesis. In this view, mathematics progresses in a sort of linear fashion and we all contribute to get from point $A$ to point $B$. In reality, solutions of these problems have been attempted by so many people that an “average” mathematician cannot expect to push the needle. It is a lot easier to invent a new problem than to solve an existing one. This is why we consider variations, analogs and generalizations. Mathematics keeps branching out but not necessarily progressing — we are wading in the shallows without ever venturing out on the open sea. It also leads to the crumbling of the mathematical community (here it comes to mind that I could write an entire separate post about how much I dislike the word “community”) into small groups working separately and having only the vaguest idea of what's going on with the others. To illustrate this point, there were 35 new preprints posted on arXiv today (plus 19 revisions) in the number theory subcategory alone. Based on a quick Google search, there were over 4000 posts in number theory in 2025. How many of them have I actually read from start to finish? Maybe 2. This creates a gulf between supply and demand, a situation where everybody keeps churning out papers but nobody wants to read them.

There are reasons for this behavior, and they have everything to do with securing a job in academia and funding. This is where I think someone working in the US system might have a blind spot. Having graduated with a PhD only in September 2025, I cannot claim to see clearly into the shadows surrounding the politics of scientific funding in the Czech Republic (to take the example of my home country). One of the principles at play is that the amount of funding a university receives from the government depends on the university's publication output. Moreover, the quantity of publications matters much more than the quality. The university therefore has an incentive to hire someone who can be expected to write a lot of papers, which makes the number of publications the most important metric on the candidate's CV. A researcher might find themselves in a position were they prove a result they feel is suboptimal and could potentially be improved with extra work. Then they face the choice to either spend a few more weeks/months proving the stronger version and have the hope of getting it published in a better journal, or quickly write up the weaker version and move on to something else. Of course they are likely to choose the latter, especially since getting the paper published in a better journal just because it contains a stronger result is not at all guaranteed. Besides, if the stronger version is subsequently proved by someone else, it will bring them more citations. I speak from experience: My advisor pushed me several times to just put up a preprint on arXiv instead of spending more time on it.

When someone says that the goal of mathematics is human understanding, they should remember all the times their colleagues (if not, ehm, they themselves) decided to crank out yet another mediocre paper instead of helping a struggling student or better preparing for their next lecture. [The purpose of a system is what it does](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_purpose_of_a_system_is_what_it_does). They may come to feel — as I have felt doing a PhD and now a postdoc — that mathematics was in crisis long before AI.
